# Prototyping as a core skill for designers

Prototyping is one of those skills for designers which can make one stand out from the crowd, because it allows you to turn ideas into working, tangible artefacts without help from anyone else.

It feels like a superpower when you can make things work and feel real. You feel 10x more efficient and confident with your workflows and also start getting an idea of how things are built.

In this article, I’ve tried to list down some of my learnings and thought process since I started prototyping. Knowing your tools Translating ideas to reality always requires a medium or a tool. While the underlying fundamentals are mostly same for different tools, it is important that you pick the right tool for yourself. And picking the right tool should not happen based on complexity levels, rather it should be based on what that tool enables for you. That medium could be code or any canvas based design tool

I for instance use Codex, Cursor and Play depending on my use case. Approaching a prototype Whenever I build some prototype, I usually try to break it down in terms of interactive and supporting elements. There would be some main components in the interface which should work exactly how you’d want it to be and then there are some elements which just need to be there in order to create an illusion of a real prototype. For example, if I were to make a drag a drop interaction, I would spend time in polishing it to detail.

And assuming there’s a card alongside with some other content in it, I would just import that as a static image. This also helps in cutting down time for building prototypes.

> A prototype is like a movie set. Not everything has to be real in it. There are often some elements which are just secondary in nature.

I read this in “Creative selection” by Ken Kocienda where he talks about prototyping and demo culture at apple

Why care about prototyping? 1. Good prototypes helps you sell better : When you present a prototype or share it with stakeholders, it makes the idea real and tangible for them. It doesn’t have to fully functional with every action working because everyone including you knows that its a prototype. No one expects it to be a fully functional product. But if its well polished, it creates an illusion of a real product. It show the direction, thought process and next steps much more clearly. Having a functional prototype which feels real helps people visualise things better.

Static screens on Figma helps understand the flows, but its boring to go through static screens and it’s also hard to present. Using and presenting a prototype on the device you’re designing for, helps form a better opinion of what’s good or bad, whats working and not working.

2\. Prototyping allows for better design handoffs : Making designs and entire flows on Figma is important, but communicating how should an element behave when state changes, how the top navigation becomes sticky on scroll, how does a stepper interaction work etc, all of these details can’t just be a note in a Figma file while doing the handoff.

With a prototype as reference, the dev handoff becomes much more seamless. It removes any ambiguity and helps set the direction right.

3\. Prototyping is a superpower : When you’re able to make the product feel real and work how you want it to be, you get a better sense of how people would actually perceive it very early in the product development journey. Moreover you won’t feel dependent on someone else to do the job of building.

Once you get a good hang of prototyping, the lines between static designs and functional prototypes gets blurred. At times, I just prefer building things directly without actually designing static mocks first. And it just makes you very efficient. What makes for good interactions

1.Familiarity : Most of the good interactions are usually based on the natural human behaviour and physics based movements which we often see around in our day to day life outside the digital screens.

Tinder’s swipe interaction is a classic example of a real human behaviour based interaction. That swipe gesture is directly related to how we flick things off to sides when like or don’t like something. You don’t necessarily have to create something unique every time. Use existing patterns and behaviours or just try to build on top of them.

2\. Fluidity : Interactions can become boring when not given much thought into and when they are just done for the sake of it.

State transition, timings, easing curves, small intentional delays etc plays an important part in making an interaction feel seamless and fluid.

Most of the times its just about tweaking the timing by few mili seconds, seeing how does an element responds on touch down or when scroll happens. Intuition and taste plays a key role here. And most importantly caring about the medium you’re designing for.

Whenever working on a prototype, I always think that how can I make it feel more alive. Something which users would love to use and interact with. Something for which user would come back on the app just to reimagine how seamless it felt last time they used it.

3\. Responsiveness : I used to think of interaction design as a transition from state A to B. Good interactions however, also responds to user actions. It provides you feedback based on your actions.

Try entering a wrong passcode when unlocking your iPhone. the input indicators along with a haptics feedback informs the user that the passcode is incorrect.

Another good example of responsive interaction is velocity based scrolling, Whenever you scroll on twitter or Instagram, scrolling on open feed is usually based on the speed or velocity of the swipe gesture that you make on the screen. The scroll will be slow if you are precisely scrolling on the feed, but if you do a long swipe gesture, the interface responds and your feed is scrolled based on the velocity of the gesture. Things I do to become better at prototyping

1\. Build concept ideas : This is something that you do need any permission for. If you have any idea, just prototype it and share it with people. Post it on your social and see what others think about it. I find this very helpful in developing the “muscle of creating”. A lot of designers straight away kill the concept ideas and limit themselves from trying things beyond what has been defined as standard.

2\. Little big details: There are some interactions that I’ve made and I keep playing with them dragging each element slowly and seeing how different elements moves and morphs and responds to the actions. Getting even the smallest of things right make a very visible change in your prototype and makes the entire product feel better overtime as the details and craft becomes more visible.

People appreciate things more when they see how so much effort has been put into crafting tiny details.

3\. Expand your tool stack : Your favourite design tool can’t do everything. If you’re good at Figma you probably can create prototypes there but in reality those Figma prototypes are no where close to what your product would feel or look like in production.

For designers, Play or protopie is way better than Figma in terms of prototyping. And its even better to build prototypes directly in code using Codex or Claude code, because the barrier to code is almost gone and you can just talk to these AI agents and build real things.

Being adaptable to learn new tools as and when required is a skill in itself. And more than learning tools, I like to think in terms of workflows and also really understanding the fundamentals from first principles.

4\. Consume good design : Find other people who are best at what you’re trying to do or learn. Consume their work, try to break down and reverse engineer their work or just reach out to them directly. I’ve created a personal repository of good interactions that I visit very often and there are designers whose work I try to study and breakdown in my free time. I sometimes even make time to just browse interactions and prototypes on Pinterest, @60fpsdesign or @Spottedinprod.

To be able to create good, you need to know and define what’s actually good and surround yourself with people doing good work to constantly raise your own ceiling. This also helps in improving your taste overtime as you keep doing more work.
